# Sources

This skill draws on the works below. You don't need to read them to use the skill. They're listed
for anyone who wants to go further, and so that the advice can be traced to where it came from.

## On writing

- **George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946).** The six rules; the catalogue of
  dying metaphors, padded verb phrases, pretentious diction and meaningless words; and the idea
  that ready-made phrases do your thinking for you. Read at
  <https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/>.
- **William Zinsser, *On Writing Well* (30th anniversary edition, 2006).** Clutter and the bracket
  exercise; writing as a transaction between two people; "the reader knows nothing"; the
  upside-down pyramid for explaining science and technology; why hedging and "simply" fail
  readers. Chapters 2–5 and 15 matter most here.
- **Benjamin Dreyer, *Dreyer's English* (2019).** The empty intensifiers and throat-clearers; the
  "nonrules" (passive voice, fragments, starting with "And" or "But") that guard against
  over-correction; reading aloud; redundant pairs ("the trimmables").
- **Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014).** The curse of knowledge, which is the difficulty
  of imagining not knowing what you know, and a modern account of classic style.
- **Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner, *Clear and Simple as the Truth* (1994).** Classic style:
  the writer has seen something and points it out to a competent reader, as an equal.
- **Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*.** Characters as subjects and
  actions as verbs; turning nominalisations back into verbs; old information before new.
- **George Gopen and Judith Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (*American Scientist*,
  1990).** Where readers expect information: context at the start of a sentence, emphasis at the
  end, and subject and verb close together.
- **William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style* (1918, public domain).** "Omit needless words."

## On technical documentation

- **Diátaxis** (<https://diataxis.fr/>). The four kinds of documentation (tutorial, how-to guide,
  reference, explanation), what each reader needs, and why mixing them fails.
- **Google developer documentation style guide** (<https://developers.google.com/style>). Voice
  and tone for technical docs: conversational without being frivolous, second person, conditions
  before instructions, no "simply" or "please" in procedures, sentence-case headings.
- **Julia Evans, "Patterns in confusing explanations"** (<https://jvns.ca/blog/confusing-explanations/>).
  Thirteen ways explanations lose readers, including outdated assumptions about what the reader
  knows, strained analogies, unrealistic examples, too many concepts at once, starting abstract,
  and "what" without "why".
- **plainlanguage.gov** (US Federal Plain Language Guidelines). Writing for the reader's task:
  short sections, "you", active voice, common words.

## On model-written prose

- **Wikipedia, "Signs of AI writing"** (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing>).
  A catalogue maintained by editors who clean up machine-written articles. It covers inflated
  significance, vocabulary that changes from one model generation to the next, unprompted
  contrasts, triads, chat residue and formatting habits. It also lists signs that are *not*
  reliable, which is useful for avoiding over-correction.
- **OpenAI, GPT-6 model guide, "Personality and writing style"**
  (<https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latest-model/gpt-6-astra.md>), and **GPT-5.6
  prompting guidance** (<https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-guidance-gpt-5p6>).
  The prompts OpenAI recommends for natural writing: prose by default, lists only for parallel
  items, the main point early, detail pitched to the reader's background, no invented compound
  labels or unprompted "X, not Y" contrasts, and reports that lead with the conclusion, then
  evidence, a caveat and the next action. They also say what to cut first: introductions,
  repetition and generic reassurance.
